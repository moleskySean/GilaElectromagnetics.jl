6 Solving the Scattering Problem

We've noted that the storing the Green function tensor is infeasible, and thus we resort to using the BTTB structure of the tensor to apply it in $\order{N\log N}$ time using $\order{N}$ memory. This is great, but it's not clear how we can use this to solve eq. (3.3), the discrete scattering problem $\vb*{\mathsf{p}}_{\text{d}} = \ab(\bm{\mathsf{I}} - \bm{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{G}_{\circ}})\vb*{\mathsf{p}}_{\text{s}}$.

6.1 Iterative Solvers

In the spirit of section 1.6, we can first approach solving the scattering problem with only matvecs (a "matvec" being the application of a matrix to a vector, i.e., the act of computing $\vb*{\mathsf{f}} = \bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\circ}\vb*{\mathsf{p}}$) through the so-called Born series. The Born series is the Taylor (Neumann for matrices) series expansion of the operator:

\[\begin{aligned} \ab(\bm{\mathsf{I}} - \bm{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{G}_{\circ}})^{-1} &= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \ab(\bm{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{G}_{\circ}})^n = \bm{\mathsf{I}} + \bm{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{G}_{\circ}} + \bm{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{G}_{\circ}}\bm{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{G}_{\circ}} + \dots \end{aligned} \tag{6.1}\]

Eq. (6.1) is technically only convergent if the spectral radius of $\bm{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{G}_{\circ}}$ is less than 1, that is if the largest eigenvalue of $\bm{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{G}_{\circ}}$ is less than 1 (in absolute value). For most real problems, this is not the case, but if we assume a material with very weak scattering (i.e., $\abs{\chi} \ll 1$) so that $\bm{\mathsf{X}}$ is small enough that $\bm{\mathsf{X}}\bm{G}_{\circ}$ is itself small, then the Born series converges and we can use it to solve the scattering problem:

\[\begin{aligned} \tilde{\vb*{\mathsf{p}}}_{\text{s}} &\approx \vb*{\mathsf{p}}_\text{d} + \bm{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{G}_{\circ}}\vb*{\mathsf{p}}_\text{d} + \bm{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{G}_{\circ}}\bm{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{G}_{\circ}}\vb*{\mathsf{p}}_\text{d} + \dots \end{aligned}\]

where we would keep adding terms until we believe the series has converged. How do we know when the series has converged though? One way is to check the norm of the residual. Define the residual vector $\vb*{\mathsf{r}} = \vb*{\mathsf{p}}_\text{d} - \ab(\bm{\mathsf{I}} -\bm{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{G}_{\circ}})\tilde{\vb*{\mathsf{p}}}_\text{s}$. If $\norm{\vb*{\mathsf{r}}} < \varepsilon$ for some threshold $\varepsilon$, then we can say the series has converged and our approximate solution $\tilde{\vb*{\mathsf{p}}}_\text{s}$ is good enough. If not, we keep adding terms to the series until it does.

This is an interesting approach, but it will not work in general. For large $\chi$, or for resonant structures, the Born series will simply not converge. Even when it does, it may take many iterations to converge, which is not ideal. One way to interpret the Born series is as a particular (fixed) polynomial over the of the Krylov space of $\bm{\mathsf{I}} - \bm{\mathsf{X}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\circ}$. The Krylov space of a matrix $\bm{\mathsf{A}}$ is defined as:

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal{K}_k(\bm{\mathsf{A}}, \vb*{\mathsf{b}}) &= \text{span}\left\{\vb*{\mathsf{b}}, \bm{\mathsf{A}}\vb*{\mathsf{b}}, \bm{\mathsf{A}}^2\vb*{\mathsf{b}}, \dots, \bm{\mathsf{A}}^{k-1}\vb*{\mathsf{b}}\right\} \end{aligned}\]

That is, it is the span of the space spanned by the first $k$ powers of $\bm{\mathsf{A}}$. The Born series was the particular polynomial $p_k\ab(\bm{\mathsf{I}} - \bm{\mathsf{X}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\circ}) = \sum_{n=0}^{k-1} \ab(\bm{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{G}_{\circ}})^n$, setting a coefficient of 1 for each power of $\bm{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{G}_{\circ}}$ (i.e., each basis vector of the Krylov space). Since our goal is to find a vector $\vb*{\mathsf{p}}_\text{s}$ such that it's residual $\vb*{\mathsf{r}} = \vb*{\mathsf{p}}_\text{d} - \ab(\bm{\mathsf{I}} - \bm{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{G}_{\circ}})\tilde{\vb*{\mathsf{p}}}_\text{s}$ is small, we can instead try to find a polynomial $p_k\ab(\bm{\mathsf{I}} - \bm{\mathsf{X}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\circ})$ that minimizes the residual norm $\norm{\vb*{\mathsf{r}}}$. This is the idea behind Krylov subspace methods, which are a class of iterative methods for solving linear systems that build up a Krylov space and find the best polynomial over that space to minimize the residual. The GMRES (Generalized Minimal Residual) method is one such method that minimizes the inner-product norm of the residual.

Gila convention 6.1: Iterative Solvers in Gila

In Gila, we expose custom implementations of three iterative solvers: GMRES, GCRO-DR, and BiCGSTAB. GCRO-DR is the default solver used in Gila. It is a deflated version of GMRES that is more efficient for problems where we change $\bm{\mathsf{X}}$ slightly (this is the solver one might use in inverse design problems). GMRES is the most robust of the three. BiCGSTAB is included, but generally does not perform as well as GMRES for typical nanophotonic problems. Gila also exposes MixPrcRfn, a mixed-precision iterative refinement that runs the inner solve in single precision and computes the residual in double precision.

Technically, Gila's operators are agnostic to the solver used, and any iterative solver that only requires matvecs can be used (such as those in IterativeSolvers.jl). However, the solvers included in Gila are nice for the exploration of new preconditioners. Once a robust preconditioner is found, these solvers will be removed from Gila.

6.2 Preconditioning

To accelerate the convergence of the iterative solvers, we can use preconditioners. If we are trying to solve the problem $\bm{\mathsf{A}}\vb*{\mathsf{x}} = \bm{\mathsf{b}}$, then a left preconditioner is a matrix $\bm{\mathsf{M}}_l$ such that we instead solve $\bm{\mathsf{M}}_l^{-1}\bm{\mathsf{A}}\vb*{\mathsf{x}} = \bm{\mathsf{M}}_l^{-1}\bm{\mathsf{b}}$. We can also define the right preconditioner $\bm{\mathsf{M}}_r$ such that we instead solve $\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{M}}_r^{-1}\vb*{\mathsf{y}} = \bm{\mathsf{b}}$. The idea of a preconditioner is that we hope to find an operator $\bm{\mathsf{M}}_l$ or $\bm{\mathsf{M}}_r$ whose inverse is not only close to that of the inverse of $\bm{\mathsf{A}}$, but is also cheap to apply.

The Born series is an example of a preconditioner. In the limit that $\bm{\mathsf{X}}$ is a weak scatterer, then the operator $\bm{\mathsf{B}} = \bm{\mathsf{I}} + \bm{\mathsf{X}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\circ}$ is a good approximation to $\ab(\bm{\mathsf{I}} - \bm{\mathsf{X}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\circ})^{-1}$:

\[\begin{aligned} \bm{\mathsf{B}}\ab(\bm{\mathsf{I}} - \bm{\mathsf{X}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\circ}) &= \ab(\bm{\mathsf{I}} + \bm{\mathsf{X}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\circ})\ab(\bm{\mathsf{I}} - \bm{\mathsf{X}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\circ}) \\ &= \bm{\mathsf{I}} - \ab(\bm{\mathsf{X}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\circ})^2 \end{aligned}\]

which is second order in $\bm{\mathsf{X}}$ (and if $\bm{\mathsf{X}}$ is small, then this term is negligible). Unfortunately, the Born series does not work as an effective preconditioner for most problems, as it is only effective for weak scatterers, where GMRES already converges quickly.

In Gila, we have yet to find a general preconditioner that works well, but this is a current active research direction for our group.
